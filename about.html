Diagnostic & experimental method

Diagnose first.
Change one thing. Measure again.

FoldFlight Lab focuses on why a fold or paper flight behaves the way it does. We use controlled comparisons where practical, distinguish real observations from theory, and keep commercial recommendations downstream of the explanation.

01 · Diagnose

Start with the symptom

Turn, dive, stall, inconsistency, weak crease or alignment drift each narrow the first checks.

Problem before product
02 · Test

Control the comparison

Repeat trials, hold the environment steady where possible and change one primary variable at a time.

Method over anecdotes
03 · Explain

Separate evidence types

A real test result, a source-backed principle and a suggested experiment are not the same kind of claim.

Say what we know
04 · Improve

Recommend only after criteria

Paper, books and tools are useful when they solve a named job—not because a marketplace listing is popular.

Commerce comes last

What makes this a lab

We are not trying to build the largest catalog of paper-airplane designs. Our moat is diagnosis, material mechanics, repeatable tests, troubleshooting and explanations that help a reader understand what variable changed and what to try next.

Experiment integrity

If FoldFlight Lab reports a result from a physical experiment, the useful version of that claim should identify the conditions, number of trials, variable changed, measurement and observation. If no physical test occurred, we treat the content as theory, a hypothesis or a suggested test—not as measured evidence.

Commercial independence

The commercial model can help fund the work, but an affiliate page must still be useful if its buy buttons disappear. Named products link to the matching product page; broad needs use tightly scoped searches. We do not copy Amazon star ratings, customer reviews, prices or availability, and we do not let an affiliate destination become the evidence for its own recommendation.

Source hierarchy

For factual claims we prefer original or primary material, scientific and institutional sources, publishers/manufacturers for product specifications, recognized expert organizations, then reputable secondary sources. Competitors are useful for market intelligence and gap analysis, but they are not copied and should not be the sole authority for technical claims.
