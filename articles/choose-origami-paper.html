How to choose origami paper by model, size and fold behavior

Paper is part of the mechanism. A sheet that feels perfect for a crane can be wrong for a dense insect, modular unit or large wet-folded form. Start with the model, then choose the material.

2. Why GSM is useful—but incomplete

GSM measures mass per square metre. It does not directly tell you thickness, fiber structure, surface coating or bending resistance. OrigamiUSA’s paper reviews show this clearly: papers with similar GSM can have different thickness and folding behavior, while very light specialty papers can still be physically thick or strong.

Useful baseline, not lawOrigamiUSA’s kami review measured its reviewed paper around 63 gsm and identifies 15 cm as a common size. That combination is a practical learning baseline because it is manageable and widely familiar—not because 60–63 gsm is universally superior.

3. The five-second sheet test

  1. Make one sharp valley fold and align edges carefully.
  2. Open the sheet and inspect whether the crease stays crisp or springs back.
  3. Reverse the same crease and feel how much resistance it gives.
  4. Look for coating cracks, white stress marks or fiber damage.
  5. Fold four layers together and check whether bulk begins to move the alignment.

This tiny test gives you a direct signal about memory, surface durability and multilayer behavior before committing to a large pack.

4. Choose size with complexity

For general learning, 15 cm squares are convenient. Move to larger sheets when the sequence becomes dense, when fingers need more working room, or when small alignment errors are becoming a large percentage of the model. A larger sheet can also become more flexible, so scale changes both ergonomics and structure.

6. What not to buy yet

If you cannot explain why the current paper is limiting the model, a more exotic paper is unlikely to fix the core problem. Many failures are alignment, sequence-reading or scale problems. Use a familiar baseline first.
